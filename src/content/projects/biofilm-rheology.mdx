Biofilms are bacterial communities held together by a self-produced extracellular
matrix (ECM). That matrix behaves like a soft, living material — it flows, it yields,
it resists — and understanding which molecules set those mechanics is the first step to
controlling biofilms in medicine and industry.

## What I did

I designed and led an independent 10-week project on the mechanics of *Bacillus
subtilis* biofilms, asking **how individual ECM components govern viscoelasticity and
yield behaviour**.

## Methods

- Engineered and characterised **four single-gene deletion mutants** against wild type.
- Ran **amplitude- and frequency-sweep rheometry** to probe storage/loss moduli and
  yielding.
- Built a reproducible **Python workflow** — data cleaning, interpolation, Welch's
  t-tests and graphing — for statistically robust comparison across 30+ datasets.

## Findings

Deleting **TasA** caused a **>75% drop in storage modulus** and a **five-fold increase
in yield strain**, identifying TasA fibres as the **primary cross-linker** of the
matrix — with PGA, EPS and BslA providing secondary mechanical support.
